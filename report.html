1. Origin

This project began with a striking feature of the supplied NV Atlas sample: its English side contains a key nominal term, while its aligned Chinese side contains a compact verbal expression. The sample pairs sentences such as “I needed protection” with 「我需要保護」 and “Mr Deeds, have you anything to say in defence of these articles?” with 「迪斯先生，你要為這些報道做出辯駁嗎？」.

Translation studies often begins from equivalence: does word X correspond to word Y? The subtitle data makes that question less stable. A nominal form may be preserved as a noun, embedded in a verb phrase, or transformed into a new verbal predicate. The data therefore offers an entry point into translation shifts as observable choices rather than abstract categories.

2. Background and gap

Vinay and Darbelnet’s model of translation procedures treats transposition as a change in grammatical category while meaning is retained. Chinese and English differ in how readily they use nominalisation: English academic and formal prose often favours abstract nouns, while Mandarin frequently prefers verbal predicates. That contrast is familiar, but it is often discussed with isolated examples.

The gap addressed here is modest but concrete: a browsable set of 500 sentence pairs where the nominal term and the Chinese verbal rendering are both explicit. The site lets a reader compare the dominant rendering of a term with its less frequent alternatives and see whether the pattern is stable across subcategories.

Research question: Which nominal forms are most often realised as Chinese verbs, and how much variation lies beneath those choices?

3. Method

The starting data was the supplied NVAtlas-AI-Agent.csv. It contains 500 unique sentence IDs, with English and Chinese sentences, an English nominal key term, and a Chinese verb field. No sentences or translations were generated to enlarge the corpus. The site preserves all 500 rows and exposes the original CSV unchanged alongside the enriched browser data.

Required fields were added without altering source or target text: source, category, human_checked, unit, key_term, and notes. Categories follow a transparent, reproducible morphological rule. Terms ending in -tion or -sion are coded as action nominalisation; -ment terms are coded as result nominalisation; all other terms are coded as state or quality nominalisation.

Important limitation: The supplied file does not document a sampling seed, candidate pool, inclusion filters, or original corpus URL. It is therefore not yet a reproducible probability sample. The source field records only the sentence ID and sample name. All 500 entries remain marked human_checked = no because the original corpus could not be re-opened for verification in this session. These are honest gaps to resolve before submission, not claims to hide.

The website is deliberately static: HTML, CSS, JavaScript, and a JSON data file. All four visualisations read from the same filtered set. Search covers both sentences, the key term, the Chinese rendering, and the sentence ID. The category and human_checked controls update every chart and the table together. Clicking a network or treemap element carries that choice back into the shared filter state.

4. Analysis

The most visible distribution is morphological. Of the 500 entries, 387 contain -tion, -sion, or -ment terms. Within that group, 317 end in -tion or -sion and 70 end in -ment; 113 belong to the remaining state or quality group. The treemap makes the imbalance immediately visible: this is not a balanced typology of all English nouns, but a corpus shaped by deverbal abstract vocabulary.

Frequency is equally uneven. “Attention” occurs 45 times and is rendered as 注意 in 42 of those entries; the other four entries use 稍息, 知道, and 重视. “Permission” occurs 17 times across five renderings, while “protection” occurs 12 times and is always rendered 保護 in this sample. The network makes the contrast legible: attention has one overwhelmingly strong link, while permission radiates into several alternatives.

Variation is not a marginal exception. Sixty-seven unique terms receive more than one Chinese rendering. “Confession,” for example, has six: 認罪, 懺悔, 招供, 承認, and 自首. This supports a cautious conclusion: nominal-to-verbal transposition is not a single mechanical operation. The verb chosen depends on speech act, register, and the surrounding clause. Word-level equivalence describes the centre of the distribution, not the whole distribution.

The heat map adds a category check. High-frequency terms cluster in the action and result groups, but frequency alone does not explain variation. Some frequent terms are stable, whereas some rare terms have several renderings. The relevant variable is contextual fit, which a purely morphological rule cannot capture. The suffix categories should therefore be treated as descriptive scaffolding, not as an explanatory theory.

5. Conclusion

The corpus supports a two-part answer. First, the sample is dominated by deverbal abstract nominals, especially action and result nominalisations. Second, the Chinese renderings are overwhelmingly verbal, but they are not uniform: repeated terms can accumulate distinct verbs according to context. The value of the explorer is that it makes both the dominant pattern and its exceptions visible in the same space.

The achievement is a working prototype: 500 traceable rows, linked search and filters, four interactive visualisation families, bilingual navigation and report text, a published codebook, and CSV export. The limitation is equally clear: provenance and manual validation are incomplete. The next version should attach a source URL and retrieval date to every row, document a seeded sample, and complete the required 50-entry spot check.

6. Reflection

The hardest part was not drawing a chart. It was deciding what the data could honestly support. A visually impressive network can imply a clean corpus when the underlying sample is undocumented. Building the interface forced me to separate three claims: what the CSV contains, what the category rule assigns, and what a human has actually verified. Keeping human_checked = no was less satisfying than filling the page with green ticks, but it is the correct representation of the evidence available.

I would do three things differently. I would write the codebook before touching the full dataset, test it on 20 to 30 rows, and log every revision. I would preserve the original corpus URL and retrieval date during collection, not reconstruct them afterwards. Finally, I would validate a random sample before building visualisations, because data quality problems should change the interface rather than appear as a footnote after it.

7. AI use declaration

Tools and model

OpenAI Codex desktop coding agent (current model: DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, as configured in this session), used through conversational instructions and local file tools.

What AI did

AI inspected the CSV, added required metadata fields, applied the suffix rule, built the static interface, wrote the draft report and codebook, and checked data counts and JavaScript syntax. It did not invent corpus sentences or translations.

What the student must do

The student must verify at least 50 entries against the original NV Atlas source, correct errors, mark those rows human_checked = yes, rewrite the codebook definitions in their own words, and confirm the sampling method and source licence. The student should also review every report claim before submission.

Example prompts used

Read the attached project brief and implement the website requirements in this repository.

Profile all 500 rows and identify a translation pattern that the data can support without inventing content.

One thing AI got wrong

An early count described 91 terms as having multiple renderings; recomputation showed that 91 counted repeated entries, while only 67 unique terms have more than one rendering. The report and homepage were corrected. This illustrates why AI-generated summaries must be checked against the data.
